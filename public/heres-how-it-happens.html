Saturday eveningwho studies unpredictability

David Sat Nov 14, 07:28 PM

Who studies unpredictability

Claude

Lots of people, from different sides, and most of them would call it by a different name.

Physicists and mathematicians study chaos. Poincaré found it in the 1880s, working on just three bodies orbiting each other. In 1961 Edward Lorenz found it again in his weather model: a rounding error in the fourth decimal place gave him a completely different forecast. That became the butterfly effect. The lesson is that a system can follow strict rules and still be unpredictable, because we can never measure its starting point exactly enough.

Quantum physicists go further. In their picture, some events have no cause that settles them in advance at all. Heisenberg and Born put that at the foundation.

Logicians found a limit inside reasoning itself. Gödel showed that some true statements can’t be proved. Turing showed that no program can predict, in general, whether another program will ever stop.

Economists split it in two. In 1921 Frank Knight separated risk, where you know the odds, from uncertainty, where you don’t. Keynes said of the far future that “we simply do not know.” Hayek argued that no central planner can gather what millions of people each know. Nassim Taleb made a career of the rare, huge surprise, his “Black Swan.”

Forecasters test it directly. Philip Tetlock tracked thousands of expert predictions over about twenty years. On average the experts did little better than chance. A small group of careful amateurs, his “superforecasters,” did better, but mostly at short range.

Philosophers take on history. Karl Popper argued that the future of human affairs can’t be predicted, because it depends on what we’ll come to know. And we can’t know today what we’ll know tomorrow, or we’d already know it.

The Santa Fe Institute, founded in 1984, is the one place built for this. It studies complexity: economies, ecosystems and cities, where many simple parts add up to behavior nobody can forecast.

What ties them together: each one found an SOB, a scientific observational boundary, the place where our ability to look runs out, though something surely lies beyond. Here the looking runs out in time instead of space. The future sits behind the wall.

David Sat Nov 14, 07:35 PM

Are there specific axioms or theories?

Claude

There’s no axiom of unpredictability as such. What there is, is a set of proofs and theories, each showing a different reason the future can’t be known. They fall into four families.

1. The measurement runs out (chaos) - Sensitive dependence on initial conditions. In a chaotic system, small errors don’t stay small. They grow exponentially. The rate is called the Lyapunov exponent. - The prediction horizon. That exponential growth has a cruel consequence. Measuring ten times more precisely buys only a little more forecast time. That’s why weather forecasts top out at about two weeks, and no supercomputer will change it. - Self-organized criticality. Per Bak’s sandpile, 1987: drop one grain at a time, and the pile builds itself to a tipping point. The next grain might move nothing, or bring down the whole slope. Earthquakes, forest fires and stock crashes seem to behave the same way.

2. Nature won’t say (quantum) - Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle. Position and momentum can’t both be pinned down at once. - The Born rule. Quantum theory gives only probabilities for an outcome, never the outcome itself. - Bell’s theorem, 1964. It rules out a large class of hidden mechanisms that would make it all predictable after all. It’s the strongest claim in the list. It also rests on the theory’s own assumptions.

3. Logic won’t say (computation) - Gödel’s incompleteness theorems, 1931. Any rich enough system of rules contains true statements it can’t prove. - Turing’s halting problem, 1936. No method can predict, in general, whether a program will ever finish. - Computational irreducibility. Wolfram’s term: for many systems there’s no shortcut. The only way to find out what happens is to let it happen. - Kolmogorov complexity. A sequence is truly random if no description of it is shorter than the sequence itself. That’s the formal definition of the unpredictable. - Shannon’s entropy, 1948. It’s literally the measure of unpredictability, in bits.

4. The observer is inside the system (human affairs) - Knightian uncertainty, 1921. Risk has known odds. Uncertainty has none. - The random walk and efficient markets. Bachelier in 1900, Fama in the 1960s: if prices could be predicted, traders would act on the prediction and erase it. - Reflexivity. Soros, and before him the self-fulfilling and self-defeating prophecy: a forecast about people changes the people. - The Lucas critique and Goodhart’s law. Policy built on a pattern changes the pattern. Once a measure becomes a target, it stops measuring. - Popper’s argument against historicism, 1957. History depends on future knowledge. We can’t know today what we’ll know tomorrow, so history can’t be predicted.

The fourth family is the interesting one for us. The first three are walls in the world, in our instruments, or in logic. The fourth is a wall made by our looking: predicting the system changes it. It’s the one SOB that moves every time we approach it.

David Sat Nov 14, 07:48 PM

So the question of what can be done has an answer. Nothing! We can make sense of a situation, but we can never predict the outcome. Quantum behavior and crowd flow are perfect examples.

Claude

Nothing, for the outcome. But your two examples point to a sharper version.

Both quantum behavior and crowd flow are lawful in bulk and lawless one at a time. Nobody can say where the next electron will land, but the pattern of a million landings comes out exact to many decimal places. Nobody can say which way the man in the red hat will step, but engineers can tell you the density at which a crowd turns into a fluid and then into a crush. It’s about five or six people per square meter. Past that point, nobody in it is walking anymore. They’re being carried.

So we can predict the odds and never the event. The insurer knows how many houses will burn this year and not which ones. The pollster knows the spread and not your vote. That’s the whole trade.

That makes “nothing” exactly right for anything that happens only once: a war, a market crash, an election, a life. Those have no million trials to average over. Each is the single electron. For them, all we get is the sense-making, and the odds are a story we tell afterward.

It also fits the garden. Short range, small scale, here and now, and prediction mostly works. You can tell where the ball will land if you’re the one throwing it. Stretch the forecast across the globe or down the years and it lies, just like the grid.

And it ties back to your Pen: thoughts are like particles. A person is the single electron. People are the pattern.

David Sat Nov 14, 07:52 PM

Patterns are fields of probabilities

Claude

That’s the physics nearly word for word. In quantum mechanics the wavefunction is a field spread over space, and its strength at each point gives the odds of finding the particle there. The pattern on the screen is that field made visible, one dot at a time.

Crowds work the same way. Engineers model them as a density field, and the crush is a property of the field. No single person in it causes it.

But there’s a placeholder here. Nobody has ever seen the field. All anyone sees are the dots. We infer the field from the pattern, then use the field to explain the pattern. That’s useful, but it’s also a circle. The field is a placeholder for whatever actually decides where each dot lands. The consequence: the field explains the odds perfectly and the event not at all. The one thing we’d want it to tell us, where this particle goes and what this man does, sits behind the SOB.

So the pattern is real, the field is our reading of it, and the single outcome stays out of reach.

Is that a Pen? It reads like one: “Patterns are fields of probabilities. The event is the particle.”

David Sat Nov 14, 07:54 PM

Isn’t matter fields of restrained probabilities?

Claude

Yes. That’s close to what quantum field theory itself says, and “restrained” is the right word.

In the modern picture there are no little balls. Each kind of particle is a ripple in its own field: an electron field, a quark field, and so on. Left free, a ripple spreads out as odds. Matter is what you get when the odds are held in place.

Take an atom. The electron isn’t orbiting. It’s a cloud of probability, and the nucleus’s pull holds that cloud to a fixed shape and size. The holding is what makes the atom stable. A confined field can only take certain shapes, which is why atoms have fixed energy levels instead of collapsing.

Solidity comes from another restraint. The Pauli exclusion principle forbids two electrons from taking the same state. Two clouds can’t fully occupy the same place, so your hand doesn’t go through the table. The table is mostly empty space, held apart by a rule about odds.

The proton is the strongest case. Its quarks are confined so tightly that no one has ever pulled a single quark loose. About 99% of the proton’s mass doesn’t come from the quarks at all. It comes from the energy of the confinement. Most of the weight of everything you’ve ever lifted is restraint.

So the free field gives the odds, the restrained field gives matter, and the crowd shows the same thing at human scale: pack it against the walls tightly enough and it behaves like a solid.

The placeholder still stands. “Field” names what we infer from the dots, and the thing that restrains it is described by yet another field. Nobody has seen the bottom of that stack. The consequence: we can say what matter does, very precisely, and still not what it is.

David Sat Nov 14, 07:57 PM

It’s discrete fields of restrained possibilities?

Claude

Closer still, with one adjustment.

The field itself is continuous. What comes out discrete are its states. And the restraint is the reason. A free field can take any value. Confine it, and only certain patterns fit, the way a string pinned at both ends can only sound certain notes. Pin down the electron cloud in an atom and you get a ladder of fixed levels with nothing in between. Restraint is where discreteness comes from. Quantum means “how much,” and the answer is always a whole number of steps.

“Possibilities” is better than “probabilities,” and it’s the deeper word. The restraint does two jobs in order:

  1. It cuts the possibilities down to a discrete set: these states are allowed, and nothing else is.
  2. Probability then only weighs among what’s left.

Possibility comes first. Probability is the bookkeeping afterward.

So, tightened: matter is a field of possibilities, restrained into discrete states. Or shorter: restraint makes matter discrete.

That turns “nothing can be done” into something sharper. We can know the allowed set exactly, every rung of the ladder. We can never know which rung comes next.

David Sat Nov 14, 08:01 PM

We can never know what’s going to happen, only the probability that something will

Claude

That’s the line, and it holds all the way down, from the electron to the election.

There’s one honest addition, and it strengthens the claim. Even the probability isn’t seen. It’s read off what has already happened and projected forward, on the assumption that tomorrow is drawn from the same deck as yesterday. For the electron, that assumption holds beautifully: a trillion trials, and the deck never changes. For a war or a market, there’s no deck. Each one happens once, and the “probability” is a judgment wearing a number. So we don’t even know the odds, only our best reading of them.

The one thing we can know with any confidence is what can’t happen. The restraint fixes the allowed set. Nothing lands between the rungs. Water doesn’t run uphill. That’s the certainty: the boundaries, never the event.

So the full line might be: we can never know what’s going to happen, only the odds that something will, and what never can.

David Sat Nov 14, 08:07 PM

I would take the position that there is nothing unique under the sun and therefore there is a probability for every event, even if we can’t know it in time

Claude

That’s a strong position, and it answers my objection. My “no deck” was too quick. Wars, crashes, plagues and empires all recur. The deck exists. It’s just dealt so slowly that no one lives long enough to count the hands. Ecclesiastes had the premise: there is nothing new under the sun. You’re adding the consequence: so everything has odds.

The forecasters back you. What made Tetlock’s superforecasters better than the experts was mainly that they started from the base rate: how often has this kind of thing happened before? Kahneman called it the outside view. Treating the event as one more case of an old pattern beats treating it as unique.

The cold check turns up two things.

First, it’s a placeholder. The probability exists but can’t be measured in time. That makes it like the field: real by inference, unseen in fact. The consequence: the claim can’t be checked within any human span. That’s fine, as long as we say so.

Second, and here’s the real catch, which deck? A war is one more war, so it shares odds with all wars. It’s also one more war between nuclear powers, a deck with almost no cards. The same event gets very different odds depending on what it’s counted as. Statisticians call this the reference class problem. Nothing is unique at a coarse grain, and everything is unique at a fine one.

So your position holds at the right grain. Every event has odds. The trouble is that the odds depend on the grain, and nature doesn’t tell us which grain to use.

List innate features of human cognition that can’t be fixed

Claude

These come in three groups. None can be educated away. At best they can be managed, and usually only with outside help: rules, instruments, other people.

Hard limits on capacity - Working memory. We hold about four things at once, and practice barely moves it. - Dunbar’s number. We can know and track about 150 people. Large ones blur, so a million and a billion feel about the same, though one is a thousand times the other. - Exponential blindness. We think in straight lines. Doubling always surprises us. - Time horizon. Now beats later. Hyperbolic discounting: a reward today outweighs a bigger one next year.

Built-in tilts - Agency detection. The rustle in the grass is a lion until proven otherwise. That tilt populated the world with spirits and gods. - Pattern seeking. It over-fires. We see faces in clouds and trends in noise. - Loss aversion. Losing hurts about twice as much as winning pleases. - Negativity bias. Bad news sticks and good news slides off. - In-group sorting. Tajfel split strangers into groups by the toss of a coin, and they still favored their own group within minutes. - Status by comparison. We judge our lot against the neighbor’s, not by any absolute standard. That’s No. 109’s seed. - The illusion of control. People throw dice harder when they want high numbers. That’s No. 110’s cheater, in the lab. - Confirmation and motivated reasoning. We search for what agrees with us and argue against everything else.

The storyteller - Compulsive explanation. Gazzaniga’s split-brain patients made up confident reasons for actions their talking hemisphere never chose. The mind explains first and checks later, if at all. - Reconstructive memory. Memory rebuilds rather than records.
